Poster P111 · Narrative synthesis

Nebulised therapies for smoke inhalation injury: mechanistic rationale, evidence gaps, and the case for an adaptive platform trial

BoardP111
Session3A–3E
DateFri 19 Jun 2026
Time12:51–12:54
LocationLevel 1 Foyer
↓ Download poster (PDF) § Jump to references

01Background

Smoke inhalation injury is a major determinant of morbidity and mortality in burn patients. Approximately 35% of burn admissions to specialist centres have inhalation injury, with a 38% subsequent pneumonia rate and an estimated +20% absolute mortality attributable to inhalation injury.

Despite this, no disease-specific therapy has proven survival benefit. Nebulised unfractionated heparin, N-acetylcysteine, and β-agonists are widely used in specialist burn centres on the basis of fragmented, underpowered, and inconclusive evidence.

02Two-hit pathophysiology

First hit (0–6 hours): chemical ciliotoxicity. Direct chemical injury to bronchial epithelium, ciliary destruction, epithelial denudation, loss of mucociliary clearance, neutrophil influx and cytokine storm.

Second hit (12–72 hours): fibrin-cast obstruction. Bronchial blood flow increases ~10-fold; plasma exudation into airway creates pulmonary coagulopathy; fibrin and cellular debris form casts; small-airway obstruction with V/Q mismatch.

This gives a clear mechanistic rationale for each candidate therapy: heparin attenuates the second-hit coagulopathy; NAC addresses oxidative injury and mucus viscosity; β-agonists augment mucociliary clearance and reduce airway smooth muscle tone.

03Aim

Synthesise contemporary preclinical, translational, and clinical evidence for nebulised heparin, NAC, and β-agonists in smoke inhalation injury — with emphasis on mechanistic plausibility, methodological limitations of existing trials, and the trial design needed to provide a definitive answer.

04Key clinical evidence

  • Desai 1998 (paediatric retrospective): nebulised heparin + NAC reduced atelectasis, reintubation, mortality.
  • Miller 2009 (adult retrospective, n=30): improved lung injury scores, oxygenation, survival.
  • Elsharnouby 2014 (dose-comparison RCT, n=29): higher heparin dose (10,000 IU) reduced LIS and ventilation duration.
  • HEPBURN trial (Glas 2014→2020): powered for 116; terminated early at n=13 due to low recruitment and trial-medication cost — direct evidence that conventional RCTs fail in this disease space.
  • Lan 2020 meta-analysis (9 studies): mortality reduction (RR 0.75), more ventilator-free days, pneumonia unchanged.
  • Hakim 2025 RCT (n=88): increased VFDs, improved PaO₂/FiO₂; no mortality difference.

05Why conventional RCTs fail here

Four structural barriers — together, fatal:

  • Low incidence. ~5,000 UK cases/year across ~25 specialist centres.
  • Heterogeneity. Severity, TBSA, timing of injury, co-morbidities.
  • High acuity. Consent and randomisation needed within hours of arrival.
  • Confounded co-intervention. Nebulised heparin is almost always given alongside NAC, salbutamol, and bronchoscopic lavage.

HEPBURN is the empirical proof: powered for 116 patients across multiple specialist centres in two countries, it closed at n=13.

The proposal

A UK-led adaptive platform trial in smoke inhalation injury, embedded across the BBA-affiliated specialist burn centre network. Master protocol, three initial domains (heparin, NAC, β-agonist), Bayesian adaptive randomisation, pre-specified futility/efficacy stopping rules. Primary outcome: 28-day ventilator-free days. One consent, one infrastructure, one statistical core; perpetual recruitment overcomes the HEPBURN failure mode.

06Conclusion

Traditional RCTs have not resolved — and arguably cannot resolve — uncertainty in the management of smoke inhalation injury. Bayesian adaptive platform trials offer a pragmatic, ethically robust pathway to definitive, practice-changing evidence in this high-mortality condition. The UK burn-care network is uniquely placed to lead this work.
